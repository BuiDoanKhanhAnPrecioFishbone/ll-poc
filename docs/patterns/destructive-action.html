Pattern · Destructive action

Three presses between a user and something they cannot undo, and each one earns its place. Components: More menu · Button · ConfirmDialog

The shape

More ▾   →   the item, in danger ink   →   a confirmation   →   it happens
StepWhat it is for
1 · Behind More ▾Distance. The action is not next to the one you press all day
2 · Danger ink in the menuRecognition, before the press
3 · The confirmationA statement of consequence, and a way out

No step is decoration. Remove the menu and you get the 69px problem back; remove the confirmation and the menu is the only guard; remove the danger ink and the menu is a list of equals.

Why three and not one

Two independent routes arrived at the same answer, which is why this is not negotiable per screen:

  • Asked for, 8 October review: "Some 'danger' or less use actions such as Deactivate, Cancel, Reprint … should be kept under a More button".
  • Measured, before that: Cancel RFQ sat 69px from Run Quotation. Nielsen Norman's work on consequential options placed beside benign ones says that is where accidental clicks come from.

The confirmation

One question, one consequence, two verbs.

PartRuleExample
Sizemd, always — it holds one sentence
TitleThe question, naming the recordCancel this RFQ?
BodyThe consequence, not the action. The title already said the action
ConfirmThe verb. Never Yes or OKCancel RFQ
DismissNames what you keepGo back, Keep Editing
Tonedanger only when the thing is destructive

The button says the verb. A dialog answered by Yes has to be re-read to find out what was agreed to — and the answer is usually read second. Cancel RFQ and Confirm RFQ are unambiguous on their own.

Dismiss is never a bare "Cancel" when the confirm is also "Cancel". Cancel RFQ beside Cancel is a coin toss.

A note on where that rule is stricter than the system's. D1 says Cancel only in dialogs, which permits a bare Cancel as the dismiss. ConfirmDialog's own rule says never a bare Cancel. In the build, two of three call sites use the default Go back and one uses Cancel — on a dialog whose confirm is Refresh Prices, where there is no ambiguity. Both readings are defensible; the one that matters is the narrow one: the dismiss may not repeat the confirm's verb.

Busy is the load-bearing half

While the confirmed action runs, both buttons are disabled and the confirm shows an indicator.

  • The indicator says something is happening.
  • The disable is what stops a second press doing it twice. Every confirmation here guards something a second press would repeat — confirm an RFQ, cancel one, spend marketplace quota — and the window between the press and the answer is exactly when a user presses again because nothing has happened yet.
  • Dismiss is disabled too. Leaving mid-write is how a record ends up confirmed with nobody watching the result.

When the three steps are too many

CaseDo this instead
Reversible, and the user can see it reversedJust do it, with an undo in the toast
Destructive but trivial — removing a row from a draftOne step: the action, no confirm
Destructive and irreversibleAll three. No exceptions

A confirmation on something reversible teaches people to dismiss confirmations, which is how the one that mattered gets dismissed too.
