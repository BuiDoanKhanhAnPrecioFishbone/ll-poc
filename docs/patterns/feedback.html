What the screen says back after you press something. Four kinds, and the colour has to agree with the words. Components: Toast · Button · banners

Four kinds, and when each one

KindSaysLivesCall sites
successIt worked4.5s, background27
errorIt did not work, and what to fix9s, foreground8
undoableIt worked, and here is the way back4.5s, with an Undo5
notImplementedThis would do X, but not in this prototype4.5s, background28

The one that was wrong, and why it has its own kind

error exists because the app used to announce failures through success.

A validation message telling you which required fields were still empty arrived on the green toast. So did "Select assembly first!". The words were right and the colour said the opposite — which is worse than saying nothing, because a user who trusts the colour carries on believing the step worked.

So a failure is a different kind, not a different string, and it gets two things the others do not:

  • 9 seconds instead of 4.5. The default is tuned for that worked — a message you can miss without cost. A message naming what you must fix before continuing is one you cannot, and the user is usually still reading the form rather than watching the corner.
  • foreground, so a screen reader announces it assertively rather than waiting its turn.

Undo is what pays for inline editing

Inline editing buys speed by removing the Save step. The price is that a mis-click is a silent commit.

undoable is how that price is paid. Without it, "saves as you go" just means "cannot be taken back".

The Undo button is a Radix Action, not a plain button: it keeps the toast alive while focus is inside it, so tabbing to Undo cannot race the timeout. A way back that disappears while you are reaching for it is not a way back.

Nothing silently does nothing

notImplemented has the most call sites of any kind — 28 — and that is the point.

A mockup full of buttons that silently do nothing is worse than one with fewer buttons: a reviewer cannot tell a missing feature from a broken one.

The wording is fixed so it cannot drift: "Not in this prototype — this would {what}.", where what completes the sentence. It is the toast half of A3 rule 5 — say what is not real.

A toast for something the user must act on is a message that will be missed. A banner for something transient is clutter that outlives its reason.

Content

  • A control says what happens; the toast confirms it in the same words. Publish → Published. Not Publish → Success.
  • An error says what to do, never Something went wrong.
  • A derived figure says it is derived. A session-only change says so.
